Here are field-grounded, low-saturation ideas for each track. None of these are the usual expo staples (no smart irrigation, no generic weather stations, no helmet detection, no smart dustbins), and none are drawn from any personal chat data — they come purely from real, observable problems in the Indian/Kerala context.

## 1. Food Security & Future Agriculture

- **Acoustic early-warning for red palm weevil** – Larvae hollow out coconut palms silently; farmers notice only when damage is severe. A solar node with a contact piezo mic + tinyML on an ESP32 listens for larval feeding sounds inside the trunk and alerts early. *Fresh angle:* almost all agri projects do soil/water; internal-pest acoustics are rarely seen.
- **Post-harvest "spoilage nose" for small storages** – India loses a huge share of produce *after* harvest, in godowns and crates. An array of low-cost CO₂/ethylene + temp-humidity sensors pinpoints the exact hotspot bin before spoilage is visible. *Fresh angle:* focuses on storage loss, not the field.
- **Adaptive wildlife crop-raid deterrent** – Boars and elephants destroy harvests overnight; static fences fail. Edge camera/seismic signature classifies the animal and escalates species-specific deterrents (light patterns, sound) instead of a simple PIR alarm. *Fresh angle:* classification + adaptive response, not just detection.
- **Pollinator health / smart hive monitor** – Declining pollinators directly cut yields. Hive weight curves + sound signatures predict swarming or colony stress days ahead for beekeepers. *Fresh angle:* ties food security to pollination, a track-most teams ignore.

## 2. Climate Resilience & Disaster Preparedness

- **Pre-monsoon drain blockage monitor** – Urban flooding is often caused by clogged drains, not just rain. Ultrasonic level difference across a drain grate + tilt (displaced cover) tells the municipality *which* drains to clean before the monsoon. *Fresh angle:* prevents floods instead of just alerting water level.
- **Hyperlocal lightning pre-strike alert** – Kerala has some of India's highest lightning fatalities; district-level warnings don't help a field worker. A low-cost atmospheric electric-field sensor gives minutes of local warning via siren/app. *Fresh angle:* minute-scale, site-specific warning is rare at student expos.
- **Bridge scour sentinel** – Floods silently erode bridge foundations; collapses seem "sudden." Vibration + tilt + depth-profiling at piers trends erosion and alerts before failure. *Fresh angle:* structural view of floods, not a water-level stick.
- **Offline LoRa mesh landslide alert** – During extreme rain, networks die exactly when alerts are needed. Village-scale mesh nodes (rain + soil moisture + tilt) relay warnings node-to-node to sirens with zero internet/cellular. *Fresh angle:* resilience of the alert system itself.

## 3. Electric Mobility & Clean Infrastructure

- **Society shared-charging load balancer** – Apartments can't add many EV points due to sanctioned load limits; this is the real adoption bottleneck. A smart panel dynamically shares available load across sockets, schedules overnight charging, and bills per user via RFID. *Fresh angle:* solves infrastructure economics, not "another charger."
- **Second-hand EV battery "stethoscope"** – Used EV/battery buyers have no quick health check and get cheated. A portable pulse-load tester estimates state-of-health and prints a QR "passport," also triaging batteries for second-life use. *Fresh angle:* market trust + circular economy.
- **Thermal runaway pre-alarm for charging hubs** – EV fires give little warning. Cell voltage-delta + off-gas (VOC/H₂) + rate-of-rise temperature detection cuts charging and alerts *before* smoke appears. *Fresh angle:* goes far beyond a temperature alarm.
- **Vehicle-to-home storm backup unit** – During storm outages, an idle e-scooter/EV battery sits unused. A safe bidirectional inverter prototype powers essential home loads with automatic switchover. *Fresh angle:* mobility asset doubles as climate resilience.

## 4. Smart Infrastructure & Industrial Safety

- **Manhole-entry guardian** – Sewer workers still die from H₂S/O₂ deficiency; checks are manual and skipped. A drop-in probe profiles gas at multiple depths *before* entry, interlocks forced ventilation, and tracks the worker inside with fall detection + rescue alarm. *Fresh angle:* tackles a deadly, recurring Indian problem — judges love this impact.
- **Scaffolding integrity monitor** – Scaffold collapses kill at construction sites. Clamp-on load + tilt sensors on key standards detect overload and uneven loading before collapse. *Fresh angle:* almost nobody does this; everyone does PPE detection.
- **Arc-fault sentinel for aging wiring** – Electrical fires start with series arcs that MCBs can't see. Current-signature ML at the distribution board detects arcing patterns and trips early. *Fresh angle:* prevents the fire instead of detecting smoke.
- **Predictive health monitor for old lifts** – Residential elevators fail unpredictably; entrapments are common. Vibration, door-timing and leveling-error sensors predict failure and alert the AMC before breakdown. *Fresh angle:* an everyday urban problem that expos ignore.

## Tips to make any of these win

- **Field-validate:** interview 5–10 real users (a coconut farmer, a sanitation supervisor, a society secretary) and quote them in your pitch.
- **Show logged data:** a week of real sensor logs beats a polished PPT.
- **Build a working demo,** even at table-top scale, with a clear failure-case demo (e.g., trigger the weevil sound with a speaker).
- **State a cost:** a believable BOM under a few thousand rupees shows real-world thinking.

To build a winning project, you need to understand exactly *how* a landslide happens. It is not just "too much rain makes the mud slide." It is a specific chain of physical events. 

Here is a clear, step-by-step breakdown of how landslides occur, especially in the context of Kerala’s Western Ghats, and **how you can detect them with technology**.

---

### 1. The Simple Analogy: The Wet Sandcastle
Think of building a sandcastle. 
* **Dry sand** crumbles and won't hold a shape (low cohesion).
* **Slightly damp sand** sticks together perfectly (optimal friction and cohesion).
* **Completely saturated sand** turns into a liquid slurry and collapses (zero friction). 

A hillside behaves the exact same way.

---

### 2. The Science: "Pore Water Pressure" & "Shear Strength"
Every slope is a battleground between two forces:
1. **Shear Strength**: The friction and root networks holding the soil in place.
2. **Shear Stress**: Gravity pulling the soil down the slope.

**How water changes the game:**
Soil is not solid; it is full of tiny gaps called "pores." When it rains heavily, water fills these pores. As the pores fill up, the water pressure inside the soil (**Pore Water Pressure**) increases. This water pressure literally pushes the soil particles apart, acting like a lubricant. 
*Result:* The friction (Shear Strength) drops to near zero. Gravity wins, and the entire top layer of soil detaches and slides down.

---

### 3. The Kerala Context: Why the Western Ghats are Vulnerable
Kerala’s geography creates a perfect storm for landslides:
* **Laterite Soil Structure:** Kerala’s soil has a porous, sponge-like top layer, but underneath it is a hard, impermeable rock layer (hardpan). When heavy rain hits, water seeps through the top layer but gets trapped on the hard rock below. It cannot drain downward, so it starts flowing *sideways* along the slope, creating a slippery "slip plane."
* **Steep Slopes:** Districts like Idukki, Wayanad, and Kottayam have naturally steep gradients, meaning gravity is already pulling hard.
* **Loss of "Natural Rebar":** Tree roots act like steel rebar in concrete, binding the soil together. Deforestation for plantations (rubber, tea) or construction removes this binding force.

---

### 4. The Hidden Killer: "Soil Piping" (The Most Important Concept for Your Project)
This is the reason why most basic landslide projects fail, and why your project can stand out.

**What is it?** 
Water doesn't always saturate the soil evenly. Sometimes, it finds a tiny crack or animal burrow in the hillside. As water rushes through this tiny hole, it erodes the soil from the *inside*, creating an underground tunnel or "pipe." 

**Why is it deadly?**
* The surface of the hill looks completely normal and stable.
* A standard surface rain gauge or surface moisture sensor will show "normal" readings.
* Suddenly, the roof of the underground tunnel collapses. This triggers a massive, instantaneous landslide. 
* *This is exactly what happened in the recent Mundakkai and Chooralmala landslides in Wayanad.* The ground gave way without the typical "slow sliding" warning signs.

---

### 5. The Human Triggers (Accelerants)
Nature sets the stage, but humans often pull the trigger:
* **Toe Cutting:** Building a house or road at the very bottom (the "toe") of a slope removes the physical support holding the hill up.
* **Unscientific Quarrying:** Blasting rocks for construction creates micro-fractures in the hillside, allowing water to penetrate deeper and faster.
* **Blocked Natural Drainage:** Building walls or roads that block the natural path of water runoff, forcing it to pool and saturate one specific area.

## 📡 SENSOR ARCHITECTURE & PLACEMENT GUIDE

### **1. PORE WATER PRESSURE SENSOR (The Most Critical Sensor)**

**Model:** MPX5010DP (Differential Pressure Sensor) or MS5803-14BA (Waterproof alternative)

**Where to Place:**
- **Depth:** 3 to 5 feet (1–1.5 meters) below ground surface
- **Installation:** Insert into a **perforated PVC pipe** (called a piezometer tube) that is buried vertically in the slope
- **Location:** Place at the **mid-slope** and **toe of the slope** (bottom), as these are the most vulnerable points

**How It Helps:**
- Measures the **actual water pressure** inside the soil pores
- Normal dry soil = ~0 kPa pressure
- Dangerous saturation = >5–8 kPa pressure (triggers alert)
- Detects the "lubrication effect" **hours before** the slide occurs
- **Why it's better than soil moisture:** Moisture sensors only tell you "wet/dry." Pressure sensors tell you "how close to failure."

**Cost:** ₹400–₹800

---

### **2. ACOUSTIC EMISSION SENSOR (Detects "Soil Piping")**

**Model:** Piezoelectric Disc Transducer (40mm diameter) + Op-Amp (LM358) for signal amplification

**Where to Place:**
- **Depth:** 2 to 4 feet deep, attached to the **side of the piezometer pipe**
- **Position:** Must have direct contact with undisturbed soil (not loose backfill)
- **Number:** 2–3 sensors at different depths to triangulate the sound source

**How It Helps:**
- Listens for **high-frequency sounds (20–100 kHz)** produced when soil particles rub against each other or collapse internally
- "Soil piping" creates a distinct **crackling/popping** acoustic signature
- Can detect internal erosion **days before** any surface movement
- The ESP32 runs a TinyML model to distinguish between normal soil sounds and dangerous "piping" sounds

**Cost:** ₹150–₹300 (Piezo disc) + ₹50 (Op-Amp)

---

### **3. TILT / INCLINOMETER SENSOR (Detects Micro-Movement)**

### 💡 The Solution: The "Master-Slave" Architecture

Instead of putting expensive, deep-buried sensors everywhere, you divide the system into two types of nodes:

#### 1. The "Master Node" (The Deep Analyzer)
* **Quantity:** Only **ONE** per vulnerable slope/micro-watershed.
* **Cost:** ~₹4,500 – ₹5,000
* **What it has:** ESP32, LoRa, Deep Piezometer (pressure), Acoustic sensor, and precise tilt sensor.
* **Placement:** Placed at the **"Toe" of the slope** (the bottom) or at a known historical weak point. *Why?* Because all water and soil movement eventually converge at the bottom. If the toe fails, the whole slope fails. This is the "80/20 rule" of landslide monitoring.

#### 2. The "Slave Nodes" (The Cheap Tripwires)
* **Quantity:** Multiple (e.g., 3 to 5) scattered higher up the slope.
* **Cost:** **Under ₹600 each**.
* **What it has:** A cheap ESP8266 (or ESP32-C3), a basic **Ball/Tilt Switch** (₹20), a cheap capacitive moisture sensor (₹150), and a LoRa module (₹400). *No deep drilling, no pressure sensors, no acoustic analysis.*
* **Placement:** Pushed just 6 inches into the ground near visible surface cracks, old landslide scars, or natural water channels.
* **How it works:** If the ground shifts even slightly, the ball tilt switch triggers. It instantly sends a 1-byte LoRa message to the Master Node: *"Slave 2 triggered."* The Master Node then wakes up its deep sensors to verify if it's a real threat or just a false alarm (like an animal walking by).

---

### 🗺️ Strategic Placement (The 80/20 Rule)

You do not blanket the land. You place sensors only at **critical failure points**:

1. **The "Toe" (Bottom of the slope):** This is where the Master Node goes. It bears the most weight and is the last line of defense.

2. **Natural Drainage Channels (Gullies):** Water funnels through these. Placing a cheap Slave Node here detects sudden water surges that erode soil rapidly.
3. **Historical Scar Zones:** Areas where small slides have happened in the past are the most likely to fail again. 
4. **Near Human Alterations:** The edge of a newly cut road or a house built on the slope (where "toe cutting" has occurred).

## 🧠 What is a "Digital Twin" in This Context?

A Digital Twin is a **live, virtual replica** of the real dam-river system running on a computer. It continuously receives real-world data (rain, water levels, tides) and runs **simulations into the future** to answer:

> *"If I open 3 shutters at 2 PM tomorrow and release 800 cumecs for 6 hours, will the downstream town of Perumbavoor flood? And will I still have enough water for power generation in December?"*

It tests thousands of "what-if" scenarios in seconds and recommends the **optimal release schedule**.

## 🏗️ Complete System Architecture

### LAYER 1: DATA INGESTION (The "Senses")

| Data Source | What It Provides | How to Get It (Student-Friendly) |
|---|---|---|
| **Rainfall Forecast** | Next 72 hours of predicted rain over the catchment area | IMD GFS data (free) or OpenWeatherMap API |

### LAYER 2: THE SIMULATION ENGINE (The "Brain")

This is the core of your Digital Twin. It has 4 sub-models:

#### A. Rainfall-to-Inflow Model (SCS Curve Number Method)
- Takes the rainfall forecast and calculates how much of that rain will actually flow into the dam.
- Not all rain reaches the dam — some is absorbed by soil, some evaporates.
- **Input:** Rainfall forecast (mm) + Soil type + Land use
- **Output:** Predicted inflow into the dam (cumecs) for the next 72 hours

#### B. Reservoir Mass Balance Model
- The fundamental equation:
  ```
  Storage(tomorrow) = Storage(today) + Inflow - Release - Evaporation
  ```
- Tracks the dam's water level hour by hour.
- **Critical thresholds:**
  - **FRL (Full Reservoir Level):** Maximum safe level. Exceeding this = dam overflows uncontrollably.
  - **Rule Curve:** The ideal water level for each month (e.g., keep below 2400 ft in July, but above 2350 ft in December for power).
  - **Dead Storage:** Minimum water that must remain for drinking water supply.

#### C. River Flood Routing (Muskingum Method)
- When you release water from the dam, it doesn't reach downstream towns instantly. It takes hours to travel.
- This model simulates **how the flood wave moves** down the river.
- **Input:** Release rate from dam + River channel geometry
- **Output:** Predicted water level at downstream towns (e.g., Perumbavoor, Aluva, Kochi) at each hour

#### D. Tidal Backwater Effect Model
- For rivers like Periyar that meet the sea near Kochi.
- During high tide, the sea pushes water back up the river, raising the water level even if the dam isn't releasing water.
- **Input:** INCOIS tidal predictions
- **Output:** Adjusted downstream water levels accounting for tidal push

### LAYER 3: THE OPTIMIZATION ENGINE (The "Decision Maker")

This is what makes your project a Digital Twin and not just a dashboard.

**The Algorithm:**
1. The system generates **thousands of possible release schedules** for the next 72 hours.
   - Example Schedule A: Release 500 cumecs at 6 AM, 800 cumecs at 2 PM
   - Example Schedule B: Release 300 cumecs continuously
   - Example Schedule C: Hold all water for 12 hours, then release 1200 cumecs
2. For each schedule, it runs the simulation (Layers 2A–2D) and calculates:
   - **Flood Risk Score:** Will any downstream town exceed its danger level? (0 = safe, 100 = catastrophic)
   - **Power Generation Score:** How much electricity will be lost if we release this water now? (in MW)
   - **Dam Safety Score:** Will the dam level exceed FRL? (Yes/No)
3. It picks the schedule with the **lowest combined risk**:
   ```
   Best Schedule = Minimize (Flood_Risk × 0.6 + Power_Loss × 0.3 + Dam_Risk × 0.1)
   ```
   *(Weights can be adjusted based on season — during peak monsoon, flood risk gets 0.8 weight)*

## ⚠️ LIMITATIONS & CHALLENGES (Be Honest with Judges)

### **1. Data Quality & Availability**
**Problem:**
- CWC and KSEB data is often **not real-time** (updates every 6-12 hours)
- Historical data may have gaps or inconsistencies
- Rainfall forecasts are **probabilistic**, not deterministic (e.g., "70% chance of 100mm rain")

**How to Address:**
- Use **ensemble forecasting** (run simulation 100 times with different rainfall scenarios)
- Build a **data validation layer** that flags anomalies (e.g., sudden 50% drop in dam level = sensor error)
- For the demo, use **cleaned historical data** and acknowledge this limitation

---

### **2. Model Accuracy & Calibration**
**Problem:**
- The Muskingum routing method is a **simplified approximation** of complex fluid dynamics
- Real rivers have irregular channels, bridges, and vegetation that affect flow
- Soil infiltration rates vary wildly across the catchment area

**How to Address:**
- **Calibrate the model** using 2018 actual flood data (adjust parameters until predicted water levels match actual)
- Add a **confidence interval** (e.g., "Predicted water level: 8.5m ± 0.8m")
- Acknowledge that this is a **decision-support tool**, not a replacement for expert judgment

---

### **3. Computational Complexity**
**Problem:**
- Running 10,000 simulations for optimization can take **minutes to hours** on a laptop
- Real-time deployment requires cloud computing

**How to Address:**
- Use **parallel processing** (Python's `multiprocessing` or `joblib`)
- Pre-compute common scenarios and cache results
- For the demo, show a **subset of 100 scenarios** (takes 10 seconds)

---

### **4. Political & Institutional Barriers**
**Problem:**
- KSEB (power generation) and Disaster Management Authority have **conflicting priorities**
- Dam operators may not trust an AI system

**How to Address:**

- Show that it **quantifies trade-offs** (e.g., "Release 500 cumecs now = 5% power loss but 40% flood risk reduction")
- Emphasize that the **final decision still rests with human operators**

### **5. Tidal & Downstream Complexity**
**Problem:**
- Rivers like Periyar are affected by **tidal backwater** from the Arabian Sea

- The model doesn't account for **flash floods** from local tributaries

**How to Address:**

## 📊 PART 1: Download & Process 2018 Idukki Dam Data

This script fetches the real historical data from the open-source Kerala Dam repository, cleans the messy string formats (e.g., "720.61m" → `720.61`), and isolates the critical August 2018 flood period.

### **Prerequisites**
```bash
pip install requests pandas matplotlib seaborn
```

### **Python Code: `process_idukki_2018.py`**
```python
import requests
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import seaborn as sns
from datetime import datetime

# 1. Define the raw GitHub URL for Idukki historical data
# Source: https://github.com/amith-vp/Kerala-Dam-Water-Levels
URL = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/amith-vp/Kerala-Dam-Water-Levels/main/historic_data/Idukki.json"

print("📥 Fetching Idukki Dam historical data...")
response = requests.get(URL)
data = response.json()

# 2. Extract and flatten the data
dam_name = data.get("name", "Idukki")
frl = float(data.get("FRL", "732").replace('m', '')) # Full Reservoir Level
records = []

for entry in data.get("data", []):
    date_str = entry.get("date")
    # Parse date (format is typically DD.MM.YYYY)
    try:
        date_obj = datetime.strptime(date_str, "%d.%m.%Y")
    except ValueError:
        continue # Skip malformed dates
        
    records.append({
        "Date": date_obj,
        "Water_Level_m": float(entry.get("waterLevel", "0").replace('m', '')),
        "Storage_Percent": float(entry.get("storagePercentage", "0").replace('%', '')),
        "Inflow_cumecs": float(entry.get("inflow", "0").replace('m³/s', '').replace(',', '')),
        "Spillway_Release_cumecs": float(entry.get("spillwayRelease", "0").replace('m³/s', '').replace(',', '')),
        "Rainfall_mm": float(entry.get("rainfall", "0").replace('mm', ''))
    })

# 3. Create DataFrame and sort by date
df = pd.DataFrame(records)
df = df.sort_values("Date").reset_index(drop=True)

# 4. Filter for the CRITICAL 2018 FLOOD PERIOD (August 1 - August 25, 2018)
df_2018_flood = df[(df["Date"] >= "2018-08-01") & (df["Date"] <= "2018-08-25")].copy()

print(f"✅ Processed {len(df_2018_flood)} days of critical 2018 flood data.")
print(df_2018_flood.head())

# 5. Visualization: The "2018 Crisis" Chart (Perfect for your Expo Poster)
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid")
fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 6))

# Plot Water Level
color = 'tab:red'
ax1.set_xlabel('Date (August 2018)', fontsize=12)
ax1.set_ylabel('Water Level (m)', color=color, fontsize=12)
ax1.plot(df_2018_flood["Date"], df_2018_flood["Water_Level_m"], color=color, marker='o', linewidth=2, label='Water Level')
```

## 🛰️ PART 2: Sentinel-1 SAR Flood Detection Workflow

Optical satellites (like regular Google Earth) are **blind** during Kerala's monsoon due to 100% cloud cover. **Sentinel-1 SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar)** penetrates clouds and rain, making it the *only* reliable way to validate flood extents during the monsoon.

We will use the **Google Earth Engine (GEE) Python API**. It is free for research/student projects, runs in the cloud (no massive downloads), and is the industry standard.

### **Prerequisites**
1. Sign up for a free Google Earth Engine account: [https://signup.earthengine.google.com/](https://signup.earthengine.google.com/)
2. Install the library:
   ```bash
   pip install earthengine-api geemap
   ```
3. Authenticate in your terminal/Jupyter notebook once:
   ```bash
   earthengine authenticate
   ```

### **Python Code: `sentinel1_flood_detection.py`**
*(Run this in a Jupyter Notebook for the best interactive map experience)*

```python
import ee
import geemap
import datetime

# 1. Initialize Earth Engine
ee.Initialize()

# 2. Define Area of Interest (AOI): Aluva / Periyar River downstream region
# (Coordinates roughly around Aluva, heavily affected in 2018)
aoi = ee.Geometry.Polygon([
    [76.35, 10.15], # Southwest
    [76.45, 10.15], # Southeast
    [76.45, 10.25], # Northeast
    [76.35, 10.25]  # Northwest
])

# 3. Define Time Windows
# Pre-flood baseline (dry/normal conditions)
pre_flood_start = '2018-07-01'
pre_flood_end = '2018-07-31'

# During-flood peak (Idukki shutters opened around Aug 10-15)
flood_start = '2018-08-10'
flood_end = '2018-08-20'

def get_sar_water_mask(start_date, end_date):
    """
    Fetches Sentinel-1 GRD data, filters for VV polarization (best for water),
    applies speckle filtering, and thresholds to find water.
    """
    # Load Sentinel-1 Collection
    collection = ee.ImageCollection('COPERNICUS/S1_GRD') \
        .filterBounds(aoi) \
        .filterDate(start_date, end_date) \
        .filter(ee.Filter.listContains('transmitterReceiverPolarisation', 'VV')) \
        .filter(ee.Filter.eq('instrumentMode', 'IW'))
    
    # Get median composite to reduce noise
    composite = collection.median().clip(aoi)
    
    # Water typically has very low backscatter in VV polarization (< -18 dB)
    # We convert the image to dB scale for easier thresholding
    # Note: S1_GRD is already in linear scale, we approximate thresholding 
    # A simple robust threshold for VV water is around 0.02 in linear scale (~ -17 dB)
    water_mask = composite.select('VV').lt(0.02)
    
    return water_mask

print("🛰️ Processing Pre-Flood Baseline...")
pre_flood_water = get_sar_water_mask(pre_flood_start, pre_flood_end)

print("🛰️ Processing During-Flood Peak...")
during_flood_water = get_sar_water_mask(flood_start, flood_end)

# 4. Calculate Flood Extent
# Flood Extent = (Water During Flood) AND NOT (Water Pre-Flood)
flood_extent = during_flood_water.And(pre_flood_water.Not())

# 5. Visualization Setup
Map = geemap.Map(center=[10.20, 76.40], zoom=12)
Map.addLayer(aoi, {'color': 'yellow'}, 'Area of Interest (Aluva)')

# Add Pre-flood water (Blue)
Map.addLayer(pre_flood_water.updateMask(pre_flood_water), {'palette': ['blue']}, 'Pre-Flood Water Bodies')

# Add Flood Extent (Red)
Map.addLayer(flood_extent.updateMask(flood_extent), {'palette': ['red']}, 'NEW Flood Extent (Aug 2018)')

# 6. Calculate Area of Inundation (in Hectares)
# Pixel area in hectares
pixel_area_ha = ee.Image.pixelArea().divide(10000)
flood_area_ha = flood_extent.multiply(pixel_area_ha).reduceRegion(
    reducer=ee.Reducer.sum(),
    geometry=aoi,
    scale=10,
    maxPixels=1e9
)

area_value = flood_area_ha.getInfo().get('constant', 0)
print(f"💧 Estimated Flooded Area in AOI: {area_value:.2f} Hectares")

# 7. Display the interactive map (Works in Jupyter Notebook)
display(Map)

# Optional: Export the flood extent map to your Google Drive
# task = ee.batch.Export.image.toDrive(

#     description='Kerala_2018_Flood_Extent',
#     folder='JalaTwin_Project',
#     region=aoi,
#     scale=10,
#     fileFormat='GeoTIFF'
# )
# task.start()
```
